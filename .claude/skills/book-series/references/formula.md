# The Comedy Engine

Reverse-engineered from "THE GARY B BOOK OF WHEN A RUNNER DISCOVERS HYROX"
(@gary_b_runs, 12-slide Instagram carousel).

This file is the *why*. If you only skim one thing, skim "The Six Laws".

---

## The premise, in one sentence

**An ordinary person discovers a hobby, and the hobby quietly eats their entire
personality, while someone who loves them watches with a cup of tea.**

That is the whole machine. Everything below is scaffolding around it.

The format is a mock vintage book — aged foxed paper, serif type, numbered
chapters, painterly illustrations — narrating a very modern affliction. The gap
between the stately Victorian packaging and the content (a man buying his sixth
headband) *is* the joke, before a single word is read.

---

## The cast

Exactly two named characters. Never add a third with a name.

**The Enthusiast** — the afflicted. Sincere, delighted, utterly without
self-awareness. He is never stupid and never a villain; he is *happy*. The
comedy comes from his total sincerity, not from mockery. In the source: Sebastian.

**The Observer** — the partner. Dry, patient, unbothered, armed with a mug. She
never shouts, never wins, never leaves. She just says the one true sentence
nobody else will. In the source: Camilla.

Both get faintly posh, faintly absurd British names. Sebastian and Camilla are
doing real comedic labour — "Dave and Sarah" would kill it stone dead. Pick names
with three or more syllables that sound like they own a Volvo:

> Sebastian · Camilla · Jasper · Felicity · Rupert · Arabella · Giles ·
> Cressida · Tarquin · Henrietta · Miles · Clementine · Barnaby · Imogen

Everyone else in the story is anonymous and functional: "a woman asks whether he
is injured", "his partner", "someone at parkrun". Anonymity keeps them as scenery.

## The chapter micro-structure

Every chapter is 5–7 short declarative lines, each on its own line. The sequence
is near-identical every time, and that repetition is *load-bearing* — the reader
learns the rhythm by chapter 3 and starts anticipating the turn.

```
1. THE BEHAVIOUR    Flat statement of the new madness.         ("Sebastian has discovered the sled push.")
2. THE ESCALATION   One or two lines of absurdly specific detail. ("He spends forty minutes pushing a large metal object across the gym.")
3. THE COST         Money, time, or dignity — stated plainly.   ("He pays £65 a month for the privilege.")
4. THE OBSERVER     She says the obvious, devastating thing.    ("Camilla suggests moving the wardrobe upstairs.")
5. THE DEFENCE      He justifies it with jargon, sincerely.     ("Sebastian explains that this is completely different.")
6. THE BUTTON       Optional final beat that lands the kill.    ("She asks him to turn the heating down.")
```

Not every chapter uses all six. Chapter 3 ends on the defence. Chapter 4 ends on
the button. Vary *which* beats you drop, never the order of the ones you keep.

### Who gets the last word

Roughly **70% Observer, 30% Enthusiast**. When she lands it, it's a kill. When he
lands it, it should be unintentionally revealing — "Sebastian says there is no
rest for a hybrid athlete" is funnier than anything she could have replied.

Occasionally she *agrees* with him, which is worse than disagreeing:

> Sebastian explains that she wouldn't understand.
> **She agrees.**

## The Six Laws

### 1. Deadpan, present tense, third person. Always.

No exclamation marks in the body. No "LOL". No winking. The narrator is a sober
Victorian biographer who finds nothing unusual here. The flatter the delivery,
the harder it lands.

> ❌ Sebastian has gone COMPLETELY mad and bought six headbands!!
> ✅ Sebastian has purchased six headbands.

### 2. Specific numbers beat vague ones. Every single time.

This is the highest-leverage rule in the whole format. Vague = not funny.
Specific = funny. There is no middle ground.

| Vague (dead) | Specific (alive) |
|---|---|
| several headbands | **six** headbands |
| a long time pushing a sled | **forty minutes** |
| expensive gym | **£65 a month** |
| a detailed plan | a **twelve-page** race strategy |
| barely any exercise | **eleven minutes** |
| mostly filming | forty-five minutes at the gym, **thirty-eight** filming, **seven** exercising |

Odd, un-round numbers are funnier than round ones. Eleven minutes. Thirty-eight
minutes. Twelve pages. Six headbands. Nothing lands on ten.

### 3. The Observer never escalates.

She has no temper. Her weapon is the mundane alternative — the thing he could do
for free, at home, like a normal person:

- He pays £65/month to push a weighted sled → *she suggests moving the wardrobe upstairs.*
- He trains shirtless to regulate body temperature in February → *she asks him to turn the heating down.*
- He is now a "hybrid athlete" → *she asks whether that means he can finally help with the shopping.*

Her lines are always domestic, always practical, always unanswerable.

### 4. The jargon must be real.

Use the hobby's genuine vocabulary, deployed with absolute seriousness.
"Compromised running", "wall-ball efficiency", "zone 2", "negative splits" are
all real HYROX terms. Insiders laugh because it's accurate; outsiders laugh
because it's gibberish. Both audiences are served by the *same line*.

Never invent silly fake jargon. The real stuff is already ridiculous.

### 5. One sincere beat, near the end.

Chapter 9, "The Race Day Transformation", is the warmth. All the behaviour that
has been mocked for eight chapters happens at once — shirt off, headband
adjusted, screaming — and the final line turns:

> **Camilla has never seen him so happy.**

This single line is why the series is affectionate instead of sneering, why it
gets shared by the people it's about, and why 155 people commented instead of
arguing. **Do not skip this beat.** A series without it is just bullying with
nice typography.

### 6. Escalate, never resolve.

The final chapter does not deliver a lesson, a comeuppance, or growth. It makes
it *worse* and announces more to come:

> THE END OF VOLUME 1
> Sebastian has found his new identity.
> Unfortunately, this is only the beginning.

## The 11-beat arc

The chapter titles are a universal template. The *beat* is fixed; the content is
whatever your topic supplies. This is the part you actually adapt.

| # | Beat | Function | HYROX instance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Discovery** | Inciting incident → instant overcommitment within hours | Hears about it at parkrun, has entered three events by teatime |
| 2 | **The New Vocabulary** | Jargon acquisition; he no longer speaks English | SkiErgs, compromised running, wall-ball efficiency |
| 3 | **The Expensive Thing** | Pays money to do something free at home | £65/month to push metal; the wardrobe remains upstairs |
| 4 | **The Appearance Change** | Body/clothing/grooming shifts to signal membership | Trains shirtless. In February. |
| 5 | **The Gear Accumulation** | Buys multiples of one trivial item, with a taxonomy | Six headbands, graded by intensity |
| 6 | **The Public Embarrassment** | Behaviour that strangers have to witness | Screaming at a rowing machine |
| 7 | **The Documentation** | Recording the hobby overtakes doing the hobby | 38 min filming, 7 min exercising, captioned NO EXCUSES |
| 8 | **The Recruitment** | He inflicts it on another human being | Twelve-page doubles strategy; partner just wants him to run faster |
| 9 | **The Event** ⭐ | The real thing. Genuine joy. **The warmth beat.** | First race, screaming, never seen him so happy |
| 10 | **The New Identity** | Post-event relabelling of the self | "I am now a hybrid athlete" |
| 11 | **The Next Obsession** | Escalation + VOLUME 1 sting | London, Manchester, Paris, New York |

### Adapting the arc

Beats 1, 2, 9, 10, 11 are structural — keep them in those positions.
Beats 3–8 can be reordered or swapped for a topic-specific beat if your subject
has an obvious one the template misses (e.g. for sourdough: "The Starter Has A
Name"; for padel: "The WhatsApp Group"; for cycling: "The Indoor Setup").

## The caption formula

Instagram caption under each slide. Two sentences, occasionally three. It
**restates the contrast** rather than explaining the joke, then 1–2 emoji.

> Forty-five minutes at the gym. Thirty-eight minutes filming. Seven minutes
> exercising. NO EXCUSES. 📱😂

> £65 a month to push heavy furniture around. Yet the wardrobe upstairs remains
> untouched. 💸😂

> Six headbands. Eleven minutes of exercise. Sweat management is apparently a
> very complicated science. 😂

Pattern: **staccato fragments → the damning number → emoji.** Captions are the
only place a small amount of energy (caps, emoji) is permitted. The page text
itself stays stone-faced.

## Failure modes

Things that kill this format, in order of how often they happen:

1. **Vague quantities.** "Lots of gear" instead of "six headbands". Fatal.
2. **Narrator winking.** Any sentence that signals the joke. The narrator must
   never appear to find this funny.
3. **Cruelty.** If the Enthusiast looks pathetic rather than delighted, you've
   lost the audience who *are* him — which is the whole audience.
4. **Observer as nag.** She must be amused and unbothered, never resentful.
5. **Skipping the warmth beat.** See Law 5.
6. **Overlong lines.** Each line is one breath. If it needs a comma splice, cut it.
7. **Fake jargon.** Insiders spot it instantly and the credibility collapses.
8. **Resolution.** He never learns. Nobody grows. It only gets worse.
